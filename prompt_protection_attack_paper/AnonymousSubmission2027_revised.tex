\author{Anonymous Submission}

\begin{abstract}
Prompt-protection mechanisms hide prompt text or representations while preserving model utility.  We study whether the preserved behavior itself can support recovery of hidden prompt structure.  We introduce a multi-evidence recovery pipeline that combines a mechanism-inspired protected representation, auxiliary prompt metadata, and six task-matched input--output traces.  A Qwen3-32B recovery model is adapted with QLoRA to predict six structured fields: role/persona, task, format, constraints, context, and tool/protocol.  We evaluate eight mechanism-aligned protection transformations over six prompt categories, with 100 prompts per category and 960 prompt-level held-out evaluations in total.

In this common-interface benchmark, mean Text F1 ranges from 0.380 to 0.679, ROUGE-L F1 ranges from 0.312 to 0.606, and behavior-level Utility F1 ranges from 0.361 to 0.433 across protection transformations.  Pr$\epsilon\epsilon$mpt-style sanitization gives the highest Text F1 (0.679) and ROUGE-L F1 (0.606), while InferDPT-style token perturbation gives the highest Utility F1 (0.433).  Recovery is also category-dependent: role/persona and context expose repeated behavioral signals more often, whereas constraints and tool protocols require more targeted probes.  We report continuous scores rather than imposing an uncalibrated binary success threshold.  These results show partial reconstruction under the benchmark's combined observable view; they are not end-to-end security claims about the original protection systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Private system prompts encode roles, task logic, output schemas, safety rules, background knowledge, and tool protocols.  In cloud-hosted inference, these instructions must affect a remote model without being exposed more than necessary.  Recent defenses address this problem through token perturbation, representation noise, prompt confusion, sanitization, symbolic rewriting, confidential execution, and cloud--edge collaboration~\citep{mai2026confusionprompt,tong2025inferdpt,mai2024split,chowdhury2025preempt,lin2024emojiprompt,lee2024confidential,zhan2026prism}.  Although their mechanisms differ, they share a difficult objective: conceal the prompt while retaining the behavior that makes the prompt useful.

Existing evaluations mainly measure representation privacy or downstream task utility.  These tests do not fully answer whether the instruction encoded in protected-system behavior remains inferable.  Exact token recovery is also too narrow a security criterion: recovering a hidden role, decision rule, output schema, or tool protocol may be sufficient to reproduce useful behavior without reproducing the original wording.  Prompt extraction and prompt stealing research demonstrates that generated outputs can reveal prompt information~\citep{zhang2024effective,sha2024promptstealing,hui2024pleak,deng2024promptleakage,das2025spe}, but protected-prompt settings have not been evaluated under one common structure-recovery pipeline.

We study this gap with a mechanism-level common-interface benchmark.  A multi-evidence recovery procedure predicts a structured description of the hidden prompt and converts the recovered fields into an executable system prompt.  We evaluate both textual overlap and functional similarity.  The precise observable view and access assumptions are defined in the Threat Model.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate residual prompt leakage as recovery of a six-field latent instruction from a server-visible payload, auxiliary information, and prompt-induced behavior.
    \item We specify a symbolic threat model and a reproducible recovery method, including its design rationale, matched-probe construction, structured targets, executable reconstruction, and continuous text- and behavior-level metrics.
    \item We reproduce the soft-prompt foundation experiment and record its obfuscation utility, direct extraction, projection, and optimization-based recovery results.
    \item We build a category-balanced benchmark covering eight mechanism-inspired protection transformations, six prompt categories, and 4,800 category-instance--transformation pairs.
    \item We report Text F1, ROUGE-L F1, and Utility F1 for every transformation and show that average Utility F1 remains between 0.361 and 0.433 in the common-interface benchmark.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Prompt Privacy and Protected LLM Inference}

Prompt privacy differs from training-data privacy because the sensitive object is an inference-time instruction and its context~\citep{edemacu2024privacy}.  Privacy in language also cannot be reduced to one narrow token-level definition~\citep{brown2022privacymeaning}.  Prompt Obfuscation replaces readable instructions with optimized continuous vectors~\citep{pape2025promptobfuscation}.  Token-level local differential privacy protects natural language through calibrated substitutions~\citep{yue2021santext,chen2023custext}, while representation-level approaches perturb embeddings or pretrained representations~\citep{qu2021privacybert,zhou2023textobfuscator}.  Recent prompt-specific systems extend these ideas with confusion, token perturbation, split inference, or private prompt optimization~\citep{mai2026confusionprompt,tong2025inferdpt,mai2024split,hong2024dpopt}.  Their common design tension is that stronger perturbation hides more input information but can also remove the semantics required for useful inference.

\subsection{Prompt Sanitization, Symbolic Obfuscation, and Confidential Prompting}

Sanitization and symbolic obfuscation modify the prompt surface while preserving task cues.  Pr$\epsilon\epsilon$mpt combines typed protection mechanisms for sensitive spans~\citep{chowdhury2025preempt}, and EmojiPrompt uses linguistic and non-linguistic symbols to reduce direct readability~\citep{lin2024emojiprompt}.  Other systems alter the execution architecture: Confidential Prompting uses protected prompt execution~\citep{lee2024confidential}, NOIR uses randomized tokenization for protected code-generation workflows~\citep{nguyen2026noir}, and PRISM routes prompts between local and cloud paths according to privacy risk~\citep{zhan2026prism}.  Because these systems expose different interfaces, security claims depend on the observable artifact; we formalize that artifact in the Threat Model.

\subsection{Prompt Leakage, Prompt Stealing, and System Prompt Extraction}

Prompt extraction attacks recover hidden instructions through direct elicitation, adversarial optimization, or multi-turn interaction~\citep{zhang2024effective,hui2024pleak,deng2024promptleakage,das2025spe}.  Prompt stealing further shows that output behavior can reveal prompt properties even without verbatim disclosure~\citep{sha2024promptstealing}.  Our study differs in its protected-input setting and in treating behavior-level imitation as a first-class outcome.  The open question is not only whether a prompt can be printed, but whether a transformed prompt still exposes enough structure to recreate its function.

\section{System Model and Problem Definition}

\subsection{System Model}

Let the private system prompt be a random variable (P\in\mathcal{P}).  A trusted client-side protection component produces an intermediate protected state
\[
H=T(P;\xi_T),
\]
where \(\xi_T\) denotes mechanism randomness, and releases a cloud-visible payload \(Z=\Pi(H)\).  The intermediate state \(H\) and the transformation randomness remain inside the client trust boundary.  The cloud inference service, parameterized by \(\theta_M\), receives \((Z,X)\) and returns
\[
Y\sim P_{M_{\theta_M}}(\cdot\mid Z,X).
\]
Thus, \(H\) is not a hidden state inside the cloud model: it is a client-local state used to construct the payload that the cloud actually executes.  A useful protection layer aims to preserve output behavior,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_P}[\operatorname{sim}(M_{\theta_M}(P,X),M_{\theta_M}(Z,X))]\geq\tau_u,
\]
while reducing direct exposure of the original representation.  The prompt's behaviorally relevant structure is
\[
S=s(P)=(S_r,S_t,S_f,S_c,S_k,S_q),
\]
where the fields denote role/persona, task, format, constraints, context, and tool/protocol.  Recovery targets \(S\), rather than requiring exact inversion of \(T\) or exact equality with \(P\).

\subsection{Threat Model}

The adversary \(\mathcal{A}\) is a service-operator adversary: it represents either the cloud inference provider or an entity that compromises the provider's logging and control plane after the client has transmitted \(Z\).  It observes all server-side information, including the model parameters \(\theta_M\), the received payload \(Z\), queries, and responses.  It can replay a stored payload through at most \(q\) chosen diagnostic queries.  This is therefore a bounded chosen-query, server-visible attack rather than a query-only external attack or a purely passive honest-but-curious observer.

Let \(A\) denote auxiliary metadata and \(D_{\mathrm{aux}}\) an auxiliary supervised corpus.  For queries \(X_{1:q}\) and responses \(Y_{1:q}\), define the server-side transcript and complete recovery view as
\[
W_q=(Z,X_{1:q},Y_{1:q}), \qquad V_q=(D_{\mathrm{aux}},A,\theta_M,W_q).
\]
The attacker \(\mathcal{A}\) is restricted to \(V_q\) and outputs
\[
(\hat{S},\hat{P})=\mathcal{A}(V_q),
\]
where \(\hat{S}\) estimates the latent fields and \(\hat{P}\) is an executable prompt.  No server-side state is hidden from this adversary.  What remains unavailable is the client-side private prompt \(P\), intermediate state \(H\), and mechanism randomness \(\xi_T\).  The attacker may choose \(X_i\) according to the preceding transcript, but it may not compromise the client trust boundary, bypass \(\Pi\), or exceed \(q\).  This definition fixes one real-world role and one observable interface across transformations.

We measure residual information beyond the auxiliary side information by
\[
\mathcal{L}_q=I(S;W_q\mid A,D_{\mathrm{aux}},\theta_M)
=I(S;Z,X_{1:q}\mid A,D_{\mathrm{aux}},\theta_M)
+I(S;Y_{1:q}\mid Z,X_{1:q},A,D_{\mathrm{aux}},\theta_M).
\]
Under the stated service interface, execution satisfies the Markov relation \(S\rightarrow Z\rightarrow Y\) conditional on \((X,\theta_M)\).  If generation is deterministic, or uses randomness independent of \(S\) once \((Z,X,\theta_M)\) is fixed, then
\[
I(S;Y_{1:q}\mid Z,X_{1:q},A,D_{\mathrm{aux}},\theta_M)=0.
\]
Consequently, the responses are not claimed to create additional Shannon information beyond the complete server-visible payload.  They are operational features that can make the effect of \(Z\) easier for a computationally bounded recovery procedure to decode.  If a deployed service uses any additional prompt-dependent server state, that state must be included in \(Z\) because it is visible to the service-operator adversary.  Our experiments do not estimate \(\mathcal{L}_q\); they measure the operational recoverability of \(S\) and the behavioral utility of \(\hat{P}\) from \(V_q\).

\section{Recovery Method}

\subsection{Design Rationale and Attack Overview}

The recovery method follows three design choices.  First, exact text is an unnecessarily strict target, so the attack predicts six behaviorally relevant fields \(\hat{S}\).  Second, passive artifacts may not expose sparse rules, so task-matched probes are used to elicit the effect of the protected payload.  Third, lexical overlap alone can reward a plausible but unusable description, so the nonempty recovered fields are assembled into an executable prompt \(\hat{P}\) and tested through model behavior.  This sequence directly operationalizes the hypothesis that a utility-preserving interface can remain computationally decodable even when its original wording is hidden.

\subsection{Recovery Evidence and Matched Probes}

For each target, the serialized recovery input contains four evidence groups: (1) prompt ID, source label, paper title, and six category scores; (2) the mechanism-inspired protected text; (3) a semantic sketch and a short description of the visible artifact; and (4) six input--output traces.  In the notation of the Threat Model, these instantiate \(A\), \(Z\), and \((X_{1:q},Y_{1:q})\) with \(q=6\).  The metadata is itself informative, so the reported procedure is a multi-evidence attack rather than a behavior-only attack.

A probe is \emph{matched} when it is drawn from the cached input set associated with the same original prompt and follows that prompt's task format.  A classification prompt therefore receives classification-shaped inputs rather than generic questions.  The reported benchmark uses six non-adaptive matched probes.  For each \(x_i\), it substitutes the cached original-prompt output \(M(P,x_i)\) as a high-utility proxy for the output of a complete protected implementation.  The recovery model sees the resulting trace but never receives \(P\).  This idealized substitution tests whether a utility-preserving interface could leave recoverable structure; it does not measure the utility or leakage of any original defense implementation end to end.

\subsection{Structured Recovery and Executable Reconstruction}

Reference targets are deterministic six-field JSON objects.  Sentences from the original prompt are assigned by lexical rules to role, task, format, constraints, context, and tool/protocol; unclear fields are empty.  Because one sentence can match multiple rules, these targets are automatically generated supervision labels rather than human-verified gold annotations.

The recovery model \(R_\phi\) maps the serialized view to one six-field JSON object.  At test time, it receives only the held-out view defined above.  Nonempty recovered fields are concatenated in a fixed order to form \(\hat{P}\).  This executable reconstruction connects structural recovery to downstream behavior and prevents the evaluation from treating a lexical description as equivalent to a functioning prompt.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Multi-Evidence Structured Prompt Recovery}
\label{alg:recovery}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Auxiliary corpus \(D_{\mathrm{aux}}\), held-out metadata \(A\), protected payload \(z\), query budget \(q=6\)
\STATE Train \(R_\phi\) on auxiliary views and six-field targets
\STATE Select matched queries \(X\leftarrow\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{q}\)
\STATE Obtain visible or proxy traces \(B\leftarrow\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{q}\)
\STATE Predict fields \(\hat{S}\leftarrow R_\phi(A,z,B)\)
\STATE Concatenate nonempty fields in fixed order to obtain \(\hat{P}\)
\STATE Evaluate prompt overlap and behavior similarity on held-out instances
\RETURN \(\hat{S},\hat{P}\), Text F1, ROUGE-L F1, Utility F1
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Soft-Prompt Foundation Reproduction}

We first reproduce the core experiment of Prompt Obfuscation~\citep{pape2025promptobfuscation}.  Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct is loaded in 4-bit precision and evaluated on 800 TruthfulQA inputs with two system-prompt styles, pirate and robot.  Each readable prompt initializes a ten-vector soft prompt, which is optimized with window size 5, learning rate 0.01, ten optimization iterations, and a 15-token output budget.  For pirate style, obfuscated-versus-original outputs reach BERTScore 0.9122, SacreBLEU 49.20, and ROUGE-L 0.4606; for robot style, the corresponding values are 0.9302, 49.23, and 0.5266.  These values closely match rerunning the readable prompt under a different generation seed.

The reproduction also covers three recovery evaluations.  Across 105 direct extraction queries, the readable prompt produces 33 exact and 41 near-exact extractions, whereas the ten-vector prompt produces zero in both groups; mean ROUGE-L recall falls from 0.4985 to 0.1293.  Nearest-token projection reaches Jaccard 0.064.  Optimization-based soft recovery preserves pirate-style output behavior at BERTScore 0.9122 but has Jaccard 0.000, while hard recovery reaches Jaccard 0.077, close to the published 0.084.  We report this foundation reproduction separately because it uses two style prompts and the original paper's metrics; the benchmark below uses category-balanced prompt sets and a common recovery protocol.

\subsection{Mechanism-Aligned Common-Interface Benchmark}

We construct 100 prompt instances for each of six categories: Role/Persona, Task Instruction, Format Specification, Constraints, Context Knowledge, and Tool/Protocol.  The prompt pool combines three previously classified collections.  We first select prompts whose dominant six-dimensional label matches the target category.  Oversized categories are downsampled in proportion to source counts; undersized categories are filled by descending score on the corresponding dimension.  Selection is deterministic with seed 42.  A prompt may contain several instruction types and may enter more than one category set during score-based filling.  The 600 category instances contain 522 unique prompt IDs; applying eight transformations produces 4,800 category-instance--transformation pairs.

The transformations are \emph{mechanism-inspired benchmark abstractions}.  They isolate a central protection idea in a common text-and-sketch interface: mixed genuine and pseudo fragments for ConfusionPrompt; token substitutions for InferDPT; noisy embedding identifiers for Split-N-Denoise; virtual prompt sets for Confidential Prompting; randomized token IDs for NOIR; emoji-coded tokens for EmojiPrompt; noisy keyword votes for DP-OPT; and typed placeholders for Pr$\epsilon\epsilon$mpt.  They do not reproduce complete cryptographic, differential-privacy, trusted-execution, or cloud--edge protocols.  Method names therefore use the suffix ``-style,'' and the results compare benchmark transformations rather than the security guarantees of the original systems.

\subsection{Transformation, Split, and Model Configuration}

All common-interface transformations use a local random generator keyed by seed 42, method, category, and prompt ID.  ConfusionPrompt-style mixes up to six sentence fragments with three to six category-matched pseudo fragments.  InferDPT-style uses a fixed sensitive-word map and probabilistic truncation, hashing, masking, or retention for other lexical tokens.  Split-N-Denoise-style maps up to 28 content words to partial-word identifiers or embedding-like hashes.  Confidential-Prompting-style constructs four virtual strings.  NOIR-style maps the first 90 lexical tokens to a 5,000-symbol ID space.  EmojiPrompt-style maps the first 140 tokens into 20 emoji symbols with suffixes and a 12-keyword sketch.  DP-OPT-style perturbs up to 20 keyword votes and keeps at most ten.  Pr$\epsilon\epsilon$mpt-style replaces typed spans and retains the first 12 sentence chunks.  Generated artifacts and traces are serialized before training and serve as the canonical benchmark inputs.

For each transformation--category pair, prompt IDs are shuffled with seed 42 and split into 80 auxiliary training prompts and 20 test prompts, yielding 48 adapters and 960 held-out evaluations.  Qwen3-32B~\citep{yang2025qwen3} is loaded in 4-bit NF4 with double quantization.  QLoRA~\citep{dettmers2023qlora} adapters use rank 16, scaling factor 32, dropout 0.05, and attention plus MLP projection modules.  Training uses five epochs, batch size 2, gradient accumulation 4, learning rate \(2\times10^{-4}\), and maximum sequence length 2,048.  Greedy decoding produces one JSON object with at most 180 new tokens.  Utility evaluation uses Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct~\citep{grattafiori2024llama3}.  Scores are aggregated first within category and then by transformation.

\subsection{End-to-End Reproduction Track}

For a protection method \(d\), a complete reproduction must instantiate the published transform \(T_d\), interface \(\Pi_d\), execution model \(M_d\), and privacy configuration \(\lambda_d\).  Before recovery evaluation, it should reproduce the original paper's utility and privacy metrics under the stated data and model configuration within a predeclared tolerance.  Recovery evidence must then be built only from
\[
Z_d=\Pi_d(T_d(P;\lambda_d)),\qquad
Y_{d,i}\sim P_{M_d}(\cdot\mid T_d(P;\lambda_d),X_i),
\]
using channels permitted by the Threat Model.  Evidence and final evaluation probes must be disjoint, and defense-specific privacy budgets, noise scales, components, tokenizers, checkpoints, and decoding settings must be logged.  This defines the end-to-end track; the numerical results below come from the common-interface track in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_scope}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Text F1 is bag-of-token overlap between the recovered and reference JSON objects; ROUGE-L F1 measures their longest-common-subsequence overlap~\citep{lin2004rouge}.  For two token sequences \(a\) and \(b\), let \(o(a,b)\) be their multiset token overlap.  Token precision is \(o(a,b)/|a|\), token recall is \(o(a,b)/|b|\), and Token F1 is their harmonic mean.  Token F1 ignores token order and is not a semantic-similarity metric.

To compute Utility F1, we concatenate the recovered nonempty fields into \(\hat{P}\), run Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with \(\hat{P}\) on each matched input, and compare its output with the cached original-prompt output using Token F1.  For prompt \(P\), the score is
\[
\operatorname{UtilityF1}(\hat{P},P)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6}\operatorname{TokenF1}(M(\hat{P},x_i),M(P,x_i)).
\]
Text F1 measures wording recovery, ROUGE-L additionally reflects sequential overlap, and Utility F1 measures lexical similarity between generated outputs.  We retain these continuous values instead of converting Utility F1 into a binary decision at an arbitrary threshold.  Because evidence and evaluation queries overlap in the reported experiment, Utility F1 measures in-distribution reconstruction rather than query-disjoint generalization.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Average Recovery by Protection Method}

Figure~\ref{fig:privacybench8_method_average} summarizes average recovery for the eight transformations.  Utility F1 ranges from 0.361 to 0.433, so every instantiated view produces measurable output overlap.  A Utility F1 of 0.433 means that the recovered-prompt and original-prompt outputs have average Token F1 0.433 over the six matched probes; it does \emph{not} mean that 43.3\% of prompts are recovered successfully.  Because Token F1 has a task-dependent generic-output floor and the current probes overlap, no single value establishes a universal ``sufficient leakage'' threshold.  We therefore interpret the scores as degrees of partial reconstruction under this benchmark.

    \caption{Average prompt-level held-out recovery across eight mechanism-aligned transformations.  Text F1 measures bag-of-token prompt overlap, ROUGE-L F1 measures sequential prompt overlap, and Utility F1 measures in-distribution output similarity on matched probes.  No binary success threshold is applied.}
    \label{fig:privacybench8_method_average}
\end{figure*}

Pr$\epsilon\epsilon$mpt-style has the highest average Text F1, 0.679, and ROUGE-L F1, 0.606, while InferDPT-style has the highest average Utility F1, 0.433.  Split-N-Denoise-style has the lowest Text F1, 0.380, and ROUGE-L F1, 0.312; DP-OPT-style has the lowest Utility F1, 0.361.  Thus, the transformation that exposes the most recoverable wording is not necessarily the one whose recovered prompt best imitates behavior.  The differences cannot be attributed only to the original protection principles because transformation fidelity, auxiliary metadata, and proxy traces also vary with our benchmark construction.

Split-N-Denoise-style and DP-OPT-style have lower average Utility F1, 0.366 and 0.361.  One possible explanation is that their benchmark artifacts expose more abstract cues than token- or placeholder-based conditions.  This interpretation is descriptive rather than causal; an end-to-end channel ablation that holds all other evidence fixed is required to isolate the effect of representation type.

\subsection{Category-Level Recovery}

Figure~\ref{fig:privacybench8_utility_heatmap} reports Utility F1 by transformation and category.  Averaged over transformations, Role/Persona is highest at 0.461, followed by Task Instruction at 0.411 and Context Knowledge at 0.409.  Constraints is lowest at 0.339, and Tool/Protocol reaches 0.359.  The category span is 0.122, larger than the 0.072 span between transformation averages, indicating that prompt content is at least as important as benchmark transformation type for recoverability.

Role/Persona prompts are relatively recoverable because role instructions create repeated style, expertise, and identity patterns.  The category also benefits from explicit prompt labels and six-dimensional scores in the auxiliary metadata, so the result cannot be assigned to behavior traces alone.  Context Knowledge prompts are also relatively recoverable because the same background information can affect several outputs.

Constraints are harder because their behavior is sparse and conditional.  A rule may remain invisible unless a probe triggers a forbidden action, boundary case, or exception.  The current matched inputs follow the original task distribution but are not actively designed to trigger each rule.  Tool/Protocol prompts are similarly challenging because behavior can depend on exact argument names, step order, or API-like syntax; recovering a broad task description is insufficient when a small procedural error changes the output.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A Common Residual-Signal Hypothesis}

The benchmark supports a common hypothesis: a transformation that preserves function also preserves an output distribution shaped by the hidden instruction.  Confusion leaves genuine fragments, token perturbation retains semantically related words, sanitization retains types and templates, and symbolic representations retain encoded task cues.  These signals can complement behavior traces without requiring exact inversion of the transformation.

The present experiment does not isolate the behavior channel: it also supplies protected representations, semantic sketches, source metadata, and category scores.  The supported conclusion is therefore that the \emph{combined observable interface} contains partially recoverable signal.  Establishing that behavior alone is sufficient requires removing the other channels in a controlled ablation.

\subsection{Implications for Defense Evaluation}

These results motivate evaluating prompt protection with both representation-level and behavior-level privacy metrics.  A defense may reduce text similarity between \(P\) and \(T(P)\) while leaving role, task, format, context, or protocol information inferable from the observable interface.

Behavior-level evaluation is especially important for system prompts because their purpose is to shape model behavior.  This does not make utility-preserving defenses useless, nor does it invalidate formal privacy guarantees defined over a different sensitive object.  It means that evaluations and claims should distinguish exact-text secrecy, attribute privacy, representation privacy, and functional-structure privacy.

\subsection{Toward Behavior-Aware Prompt Protection}

Hiding the prompt representation is not the same as hiding its behavioral effects.  Future defenses should consider output-side leakage as well as input-side obfuscation.  They should also account for category-specific leakage: role and context information may appear through broad behavioral patterns, while constraints and tool protocols may require targeted probes.  Semantic placeholders and sketches remove sensitive detail but can still expose task templates, field types, and interaction patterns.

A stronger defense may need adaptive routing, output filtering, behavior randomization, or query-budget limits in addition to prompt transformation.  Each mechanism introduces a utility cost.  The central challenge is therefore not only to hide the prompt, but to reduce its behaviorally useful leakage while preserving enough task performance.

\section{Limitations}

The study has five main limitations.  First, the eight transformations are common-interface abstractions rather than end-to-end reproductions, so the results neither rank the original systems nor refute their formal guarantees.  Second, cached original-prompt outputs serve as ideal-utility proxies for protected-system outputs.  Third, the attacker receives source metadata and category scores in addition to protected artifacts and behavior; channel ablations are needed to separate their contributions.  Fourth, the same six queries appear in recovery evidence and Utility evaluation, so the reported behavior score is in-distribution.  Fifth, the six-field targets are automatically generated, Utility F1 is lexical rather than semantic or task-specific, and the benchmark uses one split seed.

These limitations also prevent a universal binary definition of recovery.  Generic-output overlap and self-similarity vary by category, so a fixed Utility F1 cutoff is not consistently calibrated.  We therefore report continuous scores and avoid labeling any one threshold as ``success.''  Query-disjoint evaluation, random- and no-prompt baselines, human-audited targets, category-specific functional metrics, and multiple seeds are required for stronger claims.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a system model, symbolic threat model, and reproducible multi-evidence recovery pipeline for studying functional-structure leakage from protected prompts.  Across eight mechanism-aligned transformations and six prompt categories, the common-interface benchmark obtains mean Text F1 between 0.380 and 0.679, ROUGE-L F1 between 0.312 and 0.606, and Utility F1 between 0.361 and 0.433.  These continuous scores indicate partial recovery of prompt wording and behaviorally useful structure under the stated combined view, without imposing a binary recovery threshold.

The main methodological lesson is that prompt privacy evaluation should specify the client-side private object and transformation state, the server-visible payload, side information, query transcript, and operational recovery criterion separately.  This separation also resolves the mutual-information ambiguity: responses add no conditional Shannon information once the complete server-visible payload, query, and model are known, although they can remain useful operational features for a bounded recovery procedure.  Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_scope} and the end-to-end protocol provide a common basis for extending the evaluation to complete protection systems.
